Milestone 29 Review & Milestone 31 Plan

Update — 2 September

v1.90.0 was published on 31 August at 15:19 UTC and is live in all three regions. The unpublished-release warning that led this report when it was written on the morning of 31 August was resolved that afternoon. The release carried the whole backlog, including the second half of the exclusivity fix and the completed Archive action.

Two other things have moved since. Six more Milestone 29 items have closed, taking it from 73/93 (78%) to 79/96 (82%) — and three of those items were added to the milestone after it had already closed. The figures in this report are as at the closing date, which is what a sprint review measures; the drift is noted as its own point below.

This was a security and foundations sprint, and the security work was not planned. Milestone 29 closed at 78% (73 of 93 items) — 31 issues and 42 pull requests — plus 12 infrastructure changes that ran in parallel and are counted separately because they live in other repositories.

The most important thing we found, we found by accident. A routine question about how we archive customers who have stopped trading turned into an investigation that surfaced two serious defects. The worse of the two: any logged-in user, at any customer, could read another customer’s contract — term dates, minimum keg rental, and SKU pricing. Two further access-control fixes came out of a separate scope review on the documentation epic. All four are fixed and shipped.

The honest caveat: only one release reached production during the fortnight itself. v1.89.32 went out on 21 August, and nothing followed it until v1.90.0 on 31 August — three days after the sprint closed. So for the last week of the sprint, and for three days after it, finished and reviewed work sat in front of nobody. It has all shipped now, but a ten-day gap between releases is the pattern to watch, not the draft that caused it.

What we shipped

Eight themes, in the order a manager should care about them.

1. Three access-control holes found and closed Shipped

One customer could read another customer’s contract. #5928 — the endpoint that returns an exclusive agreement was checking permissions against the organisation named in the request, not the organisation that actually owns the agreement. Any ordinary user at any customer could ask for any agreement by its ID and receive it: term dates, minimum keg rental, maximum monthly keg supply percentage, annual review date, and the full SKU pricing list.

Two smaller ones came from a different route — a scope review on the documentation epic, a fortnight earlier. Operators could grant themselves permissions they should not have had on order cancel and edit (#5814 — the ticket is titled for the original visibility bug, but the self-grant fix is the substantive part), and operator-only fields were being returned to non-operators on the markets endpoint (#5819).

Why it matters: contract terms and SKU pricing are commercially sensitive. If a customer had discovered this before we did, it would have been a disclosable data breach rather than a bug fix. There is no evidence it was ever exploited — but we could not have proved that either way, which is its own argument for the logging work now on the list.

2. One bad row could take the customer list down for everyone Shipped

#5933 — the exclusivity flag on a producer was declared in code as always having a value, while the database allowed it to be empty. A single producer record with an empty flag would fail the entire customer list for every user, not just that one record’s page. The same defect made it possible to crash the customer-update endpoint with a well-formed request.

Fixed in two deliberate releases — first rejecting the bad value on the way in, then tightening the database column — so there was never a window where the two halves disagreed.

Why it matters: this is the failure mode that produces “the system is down” support calls with no obvious cause. One record, invisible in the UI, takes out a page used by everyone.

3. SonarQube replaced with checks we own 10 of 12 done

The epic (#5859) started as a support question — what is SonarQube actually doing for us? The answer was uncomfortable: its frontend analysis had been broken for weeks without anyone noticing, and nothing it reported could block a merge even when it worked. We were paying a subscription for advice that could be, and was being, ignored.

Ten of the twelve pieces are done: static analysis, security scanning, duplication detection, coverage of changed lines, and inline comments on pull requests all now run inside our own build. A separate review of that new work turned up 77 findings, which were then remediated in the same sprint (#5979).

Why it matters: the SonarCloud subscription can be cancelled once the last switch is thrown, and for the first time a failing quality check will actually stop a merge. Worth noting what we avoided: the obvious GitHub-native alternative is priced at roughly US$30 per committer per month — swapping a cheap paid service for a dearer one. This is not a saving until the subscription is actually cancelled.

4. Six order, pricing and rental corrections Shipped

A venue collection price edit was silently discarded when the new price happened to equal the line’s displayed fee (#5968). Two order filters were offering every location in the system rather than only the relevant ones — warehouses (#5935) and producer/delivery locations (#5952). The Actual Rentals view had its columns renamed to the wording the business actually uses, with thousands grouped (#5855).

Why it matters: the silent-discard pattern keeps recurring — a user makes a change, the screen accepts it, nothing is saved, and no one finds out until an invoice is wrong. That is the third one this quarter.

5. End-user documentation shipped — and it kept finding bugs Epic closed

The documentation epic (#5812) closed. Guides now exist for Rentals and Leases, Products, Batches and SKUs, Transfers, and Organisations, and they are surfaced inside the application itself rather than living in a repository (#5904). The Keggy feedback widget now files User Guide feedback as its own category, so we can tell “the docs are wrong” apart from “the product is wrong” (#5910).

Why it matters: the epic’s own title says it — “the defects it surfaced”. Writing down how a feature is meant to work is the cheapest bug-finding technique we have, and it doubles as onboarding material for new customers and new staff.

6. Production monitoring stopped crying wolf Shipped

All three of these were open and unassigned at the last review. Every failed NetSuite call was raising two identical alerts (#5745); our own metrics endpoint was returning a server error and generating an alert about itself (#5758); and product image responses were being corrupted by a PHP warning written into the image data (#5747).

Why it matters: both production faults flagged as open and unscheduled in the last report are now closed. Alert noise is what makes a team stop reading alerts.

7. The board now keeps itself up to date New

Board status — whether a piece of work is in progress, in review, or done — is now derived automatically from the branch, the pull request and the review state (#5973), rather than depending on someone remembering to drag a card.

Why it matters: every previous report has had a line about board drift. The numbers in this report, and in every future one, are only as good as the board — this removes the main reason they went wrong.

8. Billing in Konvoy Cloud started moving In progress

The billing programme now has its foundation: a dedicated read-only NetSuite integration and role, so Konvoy Cloud can read invoices without holding write credentials (#5853), plus the first invoice list endpoint. The read-model discovery is settled; the PDF retrieval spike and the design review with Finance are the next decisions.

Why it matters: this is the epic that lets customers see their own invoices and credit notes without emailing Finance. It sits in its own scope milestone (Billing in Konvoy Cloud, due 30 September), not in a sprint, so it will not show up in fortnightly completion figures.

Seven things to watch

Ordered by how quickly they need a decision.

1. The release gap — resolved, but worth a rule Closed 31 Aug

As written on the morning of 31 August, this said a week of finished work was sitting in an unpublished draft. v1.90.0 went out that afternoon and everything shipped. The underlying pattern is what remains: ten days passed between v1.89.32 and v1.90.0, spanning the end of a sprint, with reviewed work idle throughout.

Decision: agree a maximum age for a cut-but-unpublished release — two working days would have caught this — so it is a rule rather than someone noticing.

2. Seventeen carried items belong to no sprint

Milestone 29 left 20 open items at close; six have since been finished, three were added, and 17 remain. Milestone 31 was opened with 5. The carried items include two live production defects, one epic and several discovery questions. Nothing is lost — but nothing is scheduled either.

Decision: in this session, place each of the 17 into Milestone 31, into a scope milestone, or into the backlog. Anything carried three times should be closed or explicitly parked.

3. Work is still being added to a closed milestone

Three items (#5982, #5985 and #5991) were filed into Milestone 29 after the sprint had ended, moving it from 93 items to 96. All three were real work and all three landed — #5991 is the completed Archive action — so this is a bookkeeping problem, not a delivery one. But it means any milestone figure quoted later will disagree with the one quoted at the time.

Decision: once a sprint milestone closes, new work goes to the next milestone. The board automation shipped this sprint can enforce it.

4. The quality gate is built but not switched on

#5870 is the last step: making the new check required on the main branch. Until then the checks run and report but cannot block a merge — exactly the weakness we set out to fix — and we are still paying for SonarCloud alongside the replacement.

Decision: agree a cutover date this week, and a date to cancel the SonarCloud subscription after it.

5. Two silent billing defects are still open

Refill orders billing $0 (#5461) has been open 47 days and carried since Milestone 22. Newer, and the same shape: editing customer pricing silently clears the lease fees on the same row (#5889). Both fail quietly and surface as an incorrect invoice.

Decision: both into Milestone 31 at the top. These are revenue defects, not tidy-up.

6. Signing into a second account keeps the first identity

#5647, open 28 days: signing into a second account retains the previous user’s identity, and the organisation page then returns a permission error. It is a session-handling fault, and it sits uncomfortably close to the access-control work above.

Decision: schedule it with the other access-control work rather than treating it as a user-interface annoyance.

7. The infrastructure release debt has not moved since June

kubernetes #475 — the develop-to-main reconcile — was last touched on 19 June. It has appeared in three consecutive reports. Twelve infrastructure changes shipped this sprint around it, which widens rather than closes the gap.

Decision: give it an owner and a date, or close it and re-cut the branch from current state.
