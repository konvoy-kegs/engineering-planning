Infrastructure Cost Programme — Progress Review

Three boards: Databricks decommission, RDS MySQL 8.4, EKS upgrade · Progress since the 11 September report · Prepared for the CEO & COO · 6 October 2026 · All figures AUD

The AWS bill has taken its first real step down. June, July and August were each invoiced at about $10,000. September’s invoice, issued on 2 October, is $7,773 — 22% lower. The parts that are already measurable: the EKS Extended Support surcharge is gone, the database line is down a third, Databricks was winding down, and the CloudWatch trim landed. The programme’s rule is measure, then claim; this is the first month with something to claim.

Databricks is the big move this fortnight. Three weeks ago the board was 2 of 22 items done and waiting on three gates. All three were answered, the dashboard that read from it was switched off with Patrick’s and Mark’s approval, the compute was stopped on 3 October, and daily Databricks spend fell from about $24 to under $2. What remains is the irreversible part — a written go/no-go on Sunday 11 October, the data deleted the same day, and the workspaces, networks and the Airbyte feed destroyed on 15 October. Rob is doing it personally, on a weekend, one step at a time with a fresh safety check before each.

Three decisions sit outside the engineering work. First, the EKS upgrade to Kubernetes 1.35 has no start date and its recommended decision date (30 September) has passed; 1.34 leaves standard support on 2 December, and the alternative is the $2,147-a-month surcharge coming back. Second, the largest remaining lever — running everything from the Australian cluster and retiring the EU and US ones, roughly $2,700 a month on paper — is gated on two business questions nobody has answered: is there any contractual or data-residency reason to keep an EU or US presence, and is any data pipeline going to resume. Third, the RDS backup redesign carries a recorded data-loss trade-off that should be restated and confirmed, not just inherited.

Databricks decommission Board 17 · 14 of 27 closed · 52% (was 9%)

Retire all three Databricks workspaces (kc-dev, kc-prod, konvoy), their AWS networks and storage, the Airbyte pipeline that fed them, and the dashboard in Konvoy Cloud that read from them. Board · terraform #754 is the running status.

What moved since 11 September 12 items closed

Three weeks ago: 2 of 22 done, nothing could start until three gates were answered. All three were.

The gates. The one customer organisation still tied to the dashboard turned out to be Konvoy’s own internal admin account; genuine external use was two organisations and twelve requests in thirty days, and Rob recorded a GO on 18 September (konvoy #5923). Finance confirmed there is no live Snowflake contract reading the data lake — the role was last used in December 2023 (terraform #691). The Databricks subscription is pay-as-you-go with no end date and needs no receiver sign-off to stop; charges stop when usage does (#692).

The switch-off. The asset-cycle dashboard flag was turned off in production on 23 September “approved by Patrick (CEO) and Mark (COO)”, after seven days of logs showed zero customer traffic on all four analytics routes in every region (konvoy #5915). That set day zero as 2 October. On 3 October the compute was stopped — jobs paused, pipelines idle, SQL warehouses off, nothing deleted (databricks #79) — and the four Airbyte connections feeding it were set inactive, authorised by Rob (kubernetes #559). Archive copies of the data were taken to Deep Archive storage.

The safety work. The destroy path did not exist in September — the deployment workflows only knew how to build. It was wired in, pinned so it can only run deliberately, and proven three times to touch nothing on the retain list: 82, 15 and 32 resources to destroy, zero changes, zero violations, identical on 1 October and again today (terraform #690, #719, #694). The baseline was snapshotted before anything changed: about $1,560 a month of Databricks usage, plus about $230 a month of AWS networking and storage underneath it (#693).

Money: daily spend went about $43 → $24 → $9 → $3 → $1.40 between 22 September and 5 October, as measured in Cost Explorer today. On the August baseline the full teardown removes about $1,800 a month (usage plus networking); the original estimate on the board was $1,226, which the issues now say not to use. Rob’s decision on 5 October: finish the shutdown first, measure the saving afterwards — the settled bill is no longer a gate.

What happens next Irreversible steps 11 & 15 October

Every step runs only if the previous one passed. Each destroy needs a fresh plan; plans expire after 24 hours.
  1. 7 Oct — result of the Deep Archive restore test: one archived object per bucket restored and its checksum compared to the record (terraform #720).
  2. 8 Oct — second observation sitting; close the quiesce and pause items if the week is clean (databricks #80).
  3. Sun 11 Oct — third sitting, the dashboard-view test, Sentry and support check, then Rob’s written GO or NO-GO. If GO: fresh destroy plans diffed against today’s baseline; drop the kc-dev and kc-prod schemas (#81); final archive top-up with checksum spot-check; empty the two workspace root buckets (terraform #696).
  4. 15 Oct — destroy the dev and prod workspaces, then the shared layer (metastores, VPCs, NAT gateways), remove the six SSM parameters, delete the workflows (#686–#689); delete the Airbyte deployment and its database (kubernetes #560); tear down one stray network by hand — it is outside Terraform and costs about $115 a month on its own.
Needs from Rob: the written GO/NO-GO on the 11th; presence on the 11th and 15th; one account-level toggle in the Databricks console. Needs from nobody else.
The one dangerous step. The production archive prefix lives inside the shared data-lake bucket that must survive. Emptying it (terraform #696) is a prefix delete, not a bucket delete, and it is the only step on the board where a wrong path would hit retained data. It is sequenced after the restore test proves the archive is readable, and the retain-list check runs again immediately before. Worth knowing, not worth worrying about.

RDS MySQL 8.4 Board 12 · 40 of 44 closed · 91% (was 79%)

Exit MySQL 8.0 Extended Support on the four Francis database instances; the board also carries the general AWS cost items — CloudWatch, VPC endpoints, load balancers, backup retention, node right-sizing. Board · programme page.

Status Core objective done in August

All four instances upgraded 2 August, a day after the Extended Support meter started; the epic closed 7 August, 18 of 18.

The upgrade avoided an Extended Support charge the programme page records as about $1,896 a month — avoided cost, never paid, so it does not show as a reduction on the bill. What does show is the RDS line falling from $1,863 in June to $1,016 in September, from the replicas and dev clones removed around the upgrade and backup retention cut from 35 to 14 days (terraform #602). MySQL 8.4 is in standard support until July 2029.

Since 11 September, six items closed, all housekeeping. The CloudWatch trim from last sprint was measured: metric monitoring went from about $43 a month to $0; the log-ingestion half delivered nothing, so the original “50–60 a month” estimate is really about $43 (#708). A standing phantom diff on every EKS plan was eliminated (#710), the Node-NotReady alert was retuned after false positives during node rolls (#709, #749), and a Grafana Cloud key that leaked into an agent transcript was revoked and replaced the same day (#750).

Money: nothing new to book. The avoided Extended Support charge is the win; the CloudWatch item is small and measured; the right-sizing item is where the next real money is, and it is held (below).

What is open 4 items, two need a decision

  • Production node right-sizing, about $260 a month estimated (terraform #576) — held, on purpose. The clusters run roughly twice the compute they need, but that slack is also the only buffer against a spot-instance interruption, because there is no autoscaler. The sequence is: right-size what each service asks for, finish the IoT and Airbyte removals (boards 14 and 17), re-measure, then shrink. No date; it follows the 15 October teardown.
  • Francis backup redesign (#715, #716, #717) — filed 10 September, untouched since. Dev-oc to a single instance; EU and NA to a monthly backup only. The issues record a measured objection — setting nightly retention to zero destroys 15 of 16 snapshots and removes point-in-time recovery, so the worst case is up to a month of data loss in those two regions — and then record that the owner’s decision stands. Each also needs an agreed outage window per region. Dollar value is not stated.
Decision to restate: the EU/NA monthly-only backup trade-off was accepted in writing by Rob in September. Given the estate question below (both regions may be torn down anyway), it is worth a one-line confirmation in this session rather than discovering the trade-off later.

EKS upgrade & estate Board 14 · 30 of 51 closed · 59% (was 47%)

Stay off EKS Extended Support; also home to the estate-consolidation question (can everything run from prod-oc?) and the Airbyte epic, whose work is actually being done on board 17. Board · programme page.

What moved since 11 September 6 items closed

Last report: “nothing closed”. This fortnight the board caught up on the safety and hygiene items that make the next upgrade cheaper.

Production can no longer be changed by accident. All 42 jobs that apply infrastructure to production now require a deliberate, per-region, manual dispatch; in the 17 post-merge runs since, every one applied to dev and skipped prod (terraform #607). Rob considered and declined the GitHub Enterprise upgrade that would add an approval screen — about $2,900 a year — on the grounds that dispatch-as-intent is the control (#608). Node images are now pinned, so AWS cannot roll them under us; every cluster’s plan reads “no changes” for the first time (#606).

Two cost items landed. The prod-oc spot node group was halved from two nodes to one (#654, 3 October) — the general workloads are now on a single spot node with about 15% headroom, a trade-off accepted in the issue; the monthly figure was not written down at close and should be. And the first prerequisite of the 1.35 upgrade, the ingress-nginx bump, was applied to dev-oc (#641) — the issue is closed but the three production clusters have not had it yet.

Money: the 1.33 → 1.34 exit in August is the programme’s one fully measured win: $2,147 a month off the bill, visible in the EKS line above (#573). The node halving is new and unmeasured.

The 2 December date Decision overdue

Kubernetes 1.34 leaves AWS standard support on 2 December 2026. The epic recommended deciding by 30 September.

After 2 December one of two things happens: AWS upgrades the control plane on its own schedule and leaves our node groups and add-ons behind, or the Extended Support surcharge — the $2,147 a month removed in August — comes back. Reaching 1.35 buys until 27 March 2027. The chain is known and none of it has started on production: ingress-nginx on prod eu → na → oc; KEDA and cert-manager to supported versions (terraform #600); ArgoCD off an end-of-life release (kubernetes #516); then the four per-cluster hops (terraform #596, which the issue says must be split into four before work starts). 1.36 is a separate, harder problem for March: the ingress controller we use was retired by its maintainers and has no version that supports it (#601).

Decision: commit to 1.35 now and give it sprint time in October and November. Two sprints is enough if it starts in M33; starting in November is not. This is the only item on any board with a hard external deadline.

The estate question — the biggest lever, entirely a business decision 0 of 7

kubernetes #527: once the IoT fleet is retired, can everything run from the Australian cluster and the EU and US clusters be torn down?

The issue’s own arithmetic: the EU region costs about $1,500 a month and the US region about $1,215, so about $2,700 a month if both go — and a further $2,300 if Databricks and MongoDB Atlas retire with them. Two caveats the issue itself flags: most of the EU/US database instances are on reserved pricing until June 2027, so they save nothing until then, and the last consolidation sprint delivered about a quarter of what was booked. These are estimates, not plans.

None of the seven steps is engineering. The first two are questions for the business (kubernetes #530: is there any contractual or data-residency reason to keep an EU or US presence; #522: is any data pipeline going to resume). Then audits of what each region still serves and what must be kept, then a reversible teardown plan. The issue’s framing is Konvoy’s situation stated plainly: speed of cash reduction beats elegance, and do not destroy data a buyer might need.

Decision: answer the two business questions. With a yes on both, the audits are a fortnight of one person’s time and the first region could be gone before Christmas. With a no, the board closes and the right-sizing item on board 12 becomes the next lever instead.

What needs a decision

Four items. Three are not engineering questions.

1. Commit to Kubernetes 1.35 before 2 December

The recommended decision date has passed, the prerequisite chain has not started on production, and the cost of missing it is either the surcharge returning or AWS upgrading our control planes unannounced. Two sprints of part-time effort if it starts now.

Decision: yes, and it goes in M33 starting with the prod ingress-nginx bumps.

2. Answer the two estate questions

Is there any contractual or data-residency reason to keep an EU or US footprint (kubernetes #530)? Is any data pipeline going to resume (#522)? Roughly $2,700 a month on paper sits behind these two answers, although most of it only becomes real after June 2027 when the reservations lapse.

Decision: Patrick and Mark, in this session or by Friday — a yes/no on each is enough to start the audits.

3. Confirm the EU/NA backup trade-off

The backup redesign (terraform #717) accepts up to a month of data loss in EU and NA in exchange for a smaller backup bill; the objection and the overrule are both on the ticket. If those regions are being retired anyway, the redesign may be moot.

Decision: either confirm it in one line, or park the three backup issues until the estate question is answered. Rob’s call, but worth saying out loud.

4. The 11 October go/no-go

The six criteria are written in advance and it is GO only if every one passes. The data is archived and the archive is being proven restorable this week. If anything fails, the day slips a week and nothing is lost.
