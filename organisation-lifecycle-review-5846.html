Archive vs Account Status · Issue #5846 Review

Asked 17 August 2026 · Answered 25 August · Finance answers issued 31 August · One question, seven defects, eight pull requests

Patrick asked a housekeeping question and it turned out to be a real one. He wanted to tidy customers who have stopped trading out of his reports, and asked what Archive and the Account Status tick actually do. Nobody knew for certain — the answer given in the meeting was openly a guess, and it was wrong in the direction that mattered.

Archive did much less than the app claimed. Its confirmation box promised three things it did not deliver: it did not stop new orders, did not remove the customer from lists, and did not block their login. The investigation turned up nine code discrepancies, filed as seven tracked issues — including one security defect serious enough to stand on its own.

Six are fixed and shipped; one is open because it is a product decision, not a bug. The bulk archive Patrick originally asked for has deliberately not been run. Doing it under the old behaviour would have left live contract pricing attached to dead customers — 15 archived customers are in that state already.

Part 1 — The answers, as issued to finance

Four questions, answered from what the code actually does. All figures are live production as at 31 August 2026, against a total of 1,819 customers.

Q1. Does archiving a customer make them “inactive”?

No. They are two separate switches, and today they disagree for 220 customers.

Archived is its own flag, set by the Archive button. Account Status — the tick on the Overview tab — is a different, manually set “on hold” flag, normally used when an invoice is overdue.

They used to be linked: archiving forced a customer on hold. That link was deliberately removed in August, because it meant nobody could tell whether “on hold” was a real credit decision or just a side effect of archiving.

Archived15510173338

183 archived customers do not show as on hold, and 37 customers who are not archived do. A clean-up that keys off Account Status expecting it to mean “archived” will be wrong in both directions.

And to confirm the second point finance raised: any customer not archived is active in the archive sense, and whether they are exclusive has no bearing on it whatsoever. Exclusive is a third, entirely independent flag.

Q2. What happens when we archive a customer?

Nothing is deleted and nothing is lost. Every order, invoice and keg movement stays exactly as it was, and stays visible. Archiving is a status change, not a deletion.

What it does:

  • Marks the customer as archived, with an “Archived” badge.
  • Archives all of their locations as well.
  • Blocks new rental, lease and refill orders — this is new, and is one of the fixes that came out of this investigation.
  • Deliberately still allows collections and transfers, so we can get our kegs back. Blocking those would strand our fleet at a customer who has stopped paying.
  • Refuses to archive at all if the customer has open rental or lease orders.

Two things that surprise people:

  • Their access is not removed. Archiving does nothing to logins — the customer’s people can still sign in and see their history. Revoking access is a separate change we have not made, and it would affect around 260 user accounts, so it needs a deliberate decision rather than being bundled in.
  • NetSuite is not told. Archiving does not sync to NetSuite at all — there is no “archived” field on the NetSuite customer record for it to sync to. The one related field that does sync is the Exclusive tick.

What it does not do — the gaps now being fixed: it does not end exclusive pricing agreements (15 archived customers still have live contract pricing), does not clear the Exclusive tick (32 still have it set), and does not check whether they still hold our kegs (2 archived customers still hold 4 kegs).

Q3. If an archived customer comes back, can we recover everything?

Yes for the customer, no for their locations — and you do not need to create a new customer record.

The customer record and all their history are intact. There is an Un-archive action for exactly this.

The catch: their locations do not come back. Archiving archives every location; un-archiving restores only the customer. Each location has to be un-archived by hand, one at a time. For a customer with many sites that is real work, and easy to get wrong.

Restoring locations is not yet being built. The groundwork is in #5991, which starts recording which locations were archived by the archive action rather than by hand — we cannot tell those apart retrospectively today. The restore itself is a separate piece of work that has not been filed yet, and the intended design is that an admin chooses which locations to bring back rather than the system restoring all of them automatically.

Two consequences worth knowing now: the manual, one-at-a-time approach remains the only option until that work is scheduled, and even once it ships it can only help customers archived after the provenance stamp starts being written. Anyone archived before then will always need the manual approach.

Also relevant: because archiving never ended their pricing agreements, those agreements are still sitting there. That helps if the customer returns — and it is the same reason they are still being priced under contract terms while they are gone.

On Kegstar specifically: Kegstar is a competitor, so it is external to our system. Archive is the right action, and yes, they can be brought back later.

Q4. What is the “exclusive” tick linked to?

It is a label, not the pricing. It has real effects, but not the one its name suggests.

Ticking it does exactly two things: it unlocks the contracted volume forecast for that customer, and it sets the “Exclusive Customer” field on their NetSuite record. That is all.

It does not set or control any prices, does not create, start, end or extend an agreement, does not affect what the customer can order or is charged, and does not appear on invoices.

The actual commercial terms live somewhere else — in a separate, dated Exclusive Agreement record holding start and end dates, minimum volumes, casual status and agreed per-SKU pricing. That is what prices their orders. A customer is genuinely on contract pricing if they have a live agreement, regardless of what the tick says.

These four rows total 1,142, not 1,819 — the remaining 677 are customers whose tick says no and who have no agreement either, which is the case that needs no action.

So for the clean-up: do not use the Exclusive tick to decide who is on contract pricing. 46 customers would be missed and 40 wrongly included. The agreements are the source of truth; the tick is a label that has drifted from them.

On those 878 — the tick has simply never been set, so it is stored as blank rather than an explicit “no”. They read as not exclusive, which is the correct interpretation, so there is nothing to action. A change that normalises them to a definite “no” is merged, but is not yet in any pending release — the current draft release was cut two days before it landed. It needs a release cut before it reaches production.

Why the bulk archive has not run yet. The issue asked for Patrick’s list to be archived in one sweep. Under the behaviour found in the investigation that would have left live exclusive pricing agreements attached to dead customers — 15 archived customers are already in that state, and 4 of those are invisible to an end-date-only check. Archiving more customers into it would quietly have made things worse. The archive action is being completed first (#5991), then the sweep runs. This is the “say so plainly rather than archiving and hoping” clause in the issue’s own acceptance criteria being honoured.

Part 3 — The seven issues, and where each stands

In the order they were filed. Six closed; one open, and deliberately so.

#5927 — Archived and on-hold customers could still be given orders Fixed

The only guard against ordering for a held customer applied to rental orders only, and sat on three endpoints that are all deprecated. The ordering screen the application actually uses had no check of any kind — an operator could place an order for an on-hold customer, or clone an order onto an archived one, through the live interface today.

Closed 26 August · PR #5940 (+489/−23 across 7 files) · The fix is deliberately partial: rental, lease and refill are blocked, but collections and transfers still work so the fleet can be recovered. This had to land before the archive behaviour could safely change.

#5928 — One customer could read another customer’s contract Security

The endpoint returning an exclusive agreement checked permissions against the organisation named in the request, never against the organisation that owns the agreement. Any ordinary user at any customer could supply their own organisation plus an arbitrary agreement reference and receive someone else’s contract: term dates, minimum keg rental, maximum monthly supply percentage, annual review date, casual status and the full SKU pricing list.

Closed 27 August · PR #5939 · Fixed to return “not found” rather than “forbidden”, so the endpoint does not confirm that another tenant’s agreement exists. The list endpoint was checked and was never affected.

#5929 — Two pricing agreements could cover the same day Fixed

The overlap check tested whether either end of a new date range fell inside an existing agreement, but not whether the new range fully enclosed an existing one. A new agreement spanning a whole year would be accepted over an existing three-month agreement sitting inside it, and which one priced an order was then decided by a tiebreak rather than by anyone’s intent. The same flawed check was duplicated in the front-end form.

Closed 26 August · PR #5938 (+387/−5 across 5 files) · No database constraint backs this, so the fix had to land on both sides.

#5930 — Archive silently set the hold flag Fixed

The source of the 220-customer drift in Q1. Archiving flipped the account-hold flag through a plain setter that raised no event, and un-archiving never cleared it. The two flags then disagreed permanently, and nobody could tell a real credit hold from an archiving side effect.

Closed 27 August · PR #5942 (+218/−12 across 8 files) · Deliberately merged after #5927 — removing the implicit hold before the real order guard existed would have removed the incidental protection it was accidentally providing.

#5931 — None of this was documented for users Fixed

The organisations user guide was effectively silent: Account Status appeared only in a screenshot’s alt text; Archive, Hold Account and Agreements were named once as button labels in a single sentence; and the word “Exclusive” did not appear anywhere in the user guide at all. Because the guides had just been surfaced inside the application itself, that silence had become customer-facing rather than an internal gap.

Closed 25 August · PR #5937 · Directly satisfies the “raise the gap rather than archive and hope” acceptance criterion on #5846.

#5932 — There is no single “customer stopped trading” action Open — needs a decision

The root finding. Archive was never designed as offboarding; it is simply the closest thing available, which is why it keeps being asked to do work it was not built for. The open questions are for product, not engineering: should offboarding automatically end live pricing agreements, and should archived customers be hidden from the organisations list by default behind a “show archived” toggle?

Open, carried out of Milestone 29 · This is a decision, not a task — it needs Patrick and Marc, not a developer.

#5933 — One bad row could take the customer list down Fixed

The exclusivity flag was declared in code as always having a value, while the database allowed it to be empty. Because it is read while building the whole customer list, a single producer with an empty flag would have failed the entire list for every user. Confirmed latent, not a live incident — no production row was in that state — but reachable through the customer-update endpoint with a well-formed request.

Closed 29 August · PRs #5941 then #5972 · Split across two releases on purpose: reject bad values first, tighten the database column second, so code and schema were never in disagreement. This is also the change that normalises the 878 blank Exclusive ticks to a definite “no”.
