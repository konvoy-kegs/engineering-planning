Archive, Account Status & Exclusive Contract · Issue #5846 Review

Asked 17 August 2026 · Investigated 25 August · Shipped in v1.90.0 on 31 August · Follow-up answers 1 September · Figures are production, 1 September, 1,823 customers

Patrick asked a housekeeping question, and it has turned into a completed piece of work plus four open decisions. He wanted customers who have stopped trading tidied out of his reports, and asked what Archive and the Account Status tick actually do. Nobody knew for certain — the answer given in the meeting was openly a guess, and it was wrong in the direction that mattered.

Archive is now a complete action. As of v1.90.0 it refuses if there are open orders, warns about kegs still on hire, ends the customer’s exclusive agreements, clears the Exclusive Contract tick, pushes that change to NetSuite, archives their locations, and stops their people signing in. Before this work, it did almost none of that while a dialog told operators it did.

Five things now need decisions rather than engineering: whether revoking archived customers’ login should stand (it affects around 260 accounts and was bundled in rather than decided on its own merits); three screen labels that actively mislead; whether Cloud should set the Exclusive tick itself; whether to build an “End Agreement” button; and whether archived customers should drop out of the organisations list by default. Each is set out below with a recommendation.

Shipped — live now

v1.90.0 was published on 31 August 2026 at 15:19 UTC and is live in all three regions (OC, EU and NA). Everything described in Parts 1 and 2 of this document is in effect today; none of it is pending work.

Two consequences that took effect immediately and are worth knowing before anyone reports them as faults: people at archived customers can no longer sign in, and the 878 customers whose Exclusive tick had never been set now hold an explicit “no” instead of a blank. Behaviour there is unchanged — Cloud always read blank as “not exclusive” — but the data now says so plainly.

“We’ve got a heap of customers in our list of organisations, and a number of those will have either stopped trading because they’re no longer an entity, or they’re closed or insolvent, or they’ve moved to Kegstar. How do we best recognise that in Cloud? … What does archiving mean for historical information, for being able to search them, and what does the active/inactive tick box mean? What does it do?”

1. What archiving a customer does now

Seven things, plus one consequence that works differently from the rest. This is current behaviour, verified against the code running in production.

Nothing is ever deleted. Every order, invoice and keg movement stays intact and stays visible. Archiving is a status change, not a deletion — that was true before this work and it is still true.

#What archiving doesNew?
1Refuses outright if the customer has open rental or lease orders.Existing
2Warns, naming the number, if they still hold our kegs — you can then confirm or back out.New
3Marks them archived.Existing
4Ends their live exclusive agreements — end date pulled back to today, auto-extend switched off.New
5Clears the Exclusive Contract tick.New
6Archives all of their locations.Existing
7Pushes the cleared exclusive flag to NetSuite.New

It also blocks new rental, lease and refill orders, while deliberately still allowing collections and transfers — so we can get our kegs back from a customer who has stopped paying. Blocking those would strand the fleet.

The scale of what steps 4, 5, 7 and 2 were fixing. Because archive never did any of this, the backlog was already substantial when the work started: 15 archived customers still held live contract pricing, 32 still had the Exclusive tick set, and 2 archived customers still held 4 of our kegs. That is the state a bulk archive would have added to, which is why the sweep was held back until the action was complete.
The eighth consequence: their people can no longer log in. This is not something the Archive button does to them — it is checked at the moment someone tries to sign in, and Cloud refuses anyone whose only customer is archived. Two things follow. It applies to every archived customer, not just ones archived from here on, so the 338 already archived in production are covered too. And it is already in effect, as of the 31 August release — if anyone at an archived customer reports that they cannot sign in, that is why. Because it is checked at sign-in rather than applied immediately, anyone already signed in stayed signed in until their session expired, up to about an hour after the release.

This needs a decision it never got. It was bundled into the same piece of work rather than being decided on its own merits, and it affects around 260 user accounts. It is very reversible. But someone should actually choose it.

2. Un-archiving is not the inverse

The most important correction in this document. Archiving now does seven things; un-archiving undoes one of them.

Un-archiving marks them active again. That is the entire action — two lines of code. Login access does come back, because the sign-in check simply stops applying. Nothing else returns.

Lost on archiveRestored on un-archive?
Their locationsNo. Each must be un-archived by hand, one at a time. Raised as #6003 — but see the caveat below: it will only help customers archived from now on.
Their exclusive agreementsNo. The pricing stays ended and has to be re-created by hand.
The Exclusive Contract tickNo. Stays cleared.
The NetSuite exclusive flagNo. Cleared on archive, not re-pushed on un-archive.
Login accessYes. The sign-in check stops applying.
Orders, invoices, keg historyYes — never lost in the first place.

So the accurate version is: archiving loses their contract pricing and their locations; un-archiving restores neither. Their history is safe, but they come back as an ordinary non-contract customer.

One deliberate exception: an agreement that has not started yet keeps its dates, because an end date cannot sit before a start date. Only its auto-extend is switched off. That is intended, and covered by a test.

The location restore will not reach customers already archived. We cannot tell retrospectively which locations were archived by the archive action and which were archived by hand at some earlier point. v1.90.0 started stamping that provenance, so #6003 can only restore locations for customers archived from 31 August onwards. The 338 customers archived before then will always need the manual, one-at-a-time approach.
Recommendation. Leave agreements ended on un-archive — a customer returning after a year should be re-papered on current terms rather than silently resuming old pricing. But it should be visible that pricing was ended, and right now nothing tells you. Locations are the opposite case: those should come back, and that is #6003.

3. Three flags, and why the screen makes them look like one

Account Status, Active/Archived and Exclusive Contract are three independent things. The Overview tab presents them in a way that invites exactly the confusion Patrick had.

“Account Status really means On Hold — it’s just poorly labelled?”

Yes. Read exactly right. The field labelled Account Status: is the on-hold flag and nothing else. The button that changes it is honest — it says Hold Account / Unhold Account. Only the display label is vague.

There is a second reason it misleads: the tick means the opposite of every other tick on that screen. Every other yes/no row uses a tick for “yes” and a dash for “no” — Auto Invoice, Charge CC Fee, Rentals enabled, Leasing enabled, Refills enabled, Collections enabled, and Exclusive Contract. That is seven rows on the same screen. Account Status is the single exception: a padlock when the customer is on hold, a tick when they are not. So in a column of ticks, that one tick means “no” while all seven neighbours mean “yes”.

Third problem: the tick also appears where nobody has ever set the field. Blank is read as “not on hold”, so a tick means either “we decided not to hold them” or “nobody has ever looked at this” — and you cannot tell which from the screen.

Suggested fix — frontend only, cheap. Relabel the row On Hold: and render it like its neighbours: padlock for yes, dash for no. That removes the inverted logic and the ambiguity in one go.

“Is Active a separate field, or is Account Status just a label for Active?”

Two genuinely separate fields, stored in different places, and they disagree in the data today.

Archived / ActiveAccount Status (on hold)
Set byArchive / Unarchive buttonHold Account / Unhold Account button
Can be blank?No — always definitely one or the otherYes — blank for a large share of customers

They used to be linked — archiving forced a customer on hold. That was deliberately removed in August so that “on hold” always means a real credit decision rather than a side effect. As at 1 September that leaves 183 archived customers who do not show as on hold, and 37 customers who are not archived but do. Neither number has moved since 31 August.

Why the screen makes you think they are the same thing: Active is not shown on the Overview tab at all. There is no row for it. Archived status appears as a blue banner across the top of the page — “This organisation has been archived” — and by the Archive button being replaced with Unarchive. So if you scan the field list looking for Active, the only thing resembling it is Account Status. Reading them as one field is the natural conclusion. That is a gap in the screen, not a misunderstanding.

One more collision: the new filter on the organisations list is labelled Filter by Account Status, and its three options are Active (not on hold), Archived and On hold. That control covers both fields. So “Account Status” currently means on-hold-only on the Overview tab, and archived-plus-on-hold on the list screen.

Suggested fix. On Hold: for the Overview row, Filter by Status for the list filter, and add an explicit Archived: row to the Overview tab so Active has a visible home.

What the Exclusive Contract tick actually is

A label, not the pricing. It has real effects, but not the one its name suggests.

Label note: on the Overview tab it reads Exclusive Contract. Exclusive Customer is the NetSuite field it feeds.

Ticking it does exactly two things: it unlocks contracted-volume forecast entry (without it, Cloud refuses), and it sets the Exclusive Customer field on the NetSuite record. Everything else that reads it is display or audit output.

It does not set or control any prices, does not create, start, end or extend an agreement, does not affect what the customer can order or is charged, and does not appear on invoices.

The actual commercial terms live elsewhere — in a separate, dated Exclusive Agreement record holding start and end dates, minimum volumes, casual status and agreed per-SKU pricing. That is what prices their orders. A customer is genuinely on contract pricing if they have a live agreement, regardless of what the tick says.

Beyond those two effects, it is read by a couple of internal audit reports, purely as a column of output.

For any clean-up: do not use the Exclusive tick to decide who is on contract pricing — 47 customers would be missed and 42 wrongly included. The agreements are the source of truth; the tick is a label that has drifted from them, and is still drifting — see Part 4.

4. The Exclusive tick has drifted, and it is still drifting

“Do we need to tick Exclusive Customer, or can Cloud set it itself from the agreement?” — the instinct is right, nothing links them today, and it is a small change to make it so.

Creating a New Exclusive Agreement validates the dates, checks for an overlapping agreement, saves it, and notifies finance. It never touches the tick. The only two things that write the tick are someone editing it by hand, and the archive action clearing it. So today it relies on somebody remembering — which is exactly why 47 customers have contract pricing with the tick off.

This is not a historical mess that has settled — it is still accumulating. Between 31 August and 1 September, one day with no releases in between, the tick-on-but-no-pricing group went from 40 to 42 and the pricing-but-no-tick group from 46 to 47.

Recommendation: (a), and ship the auto-tick part now. It is cheap, it fixes the problem you actually have — someone forgetting — and it does not depend on fixing the bug first. Treat the auto-untick as the follow-up, done as part of Part 5, since ending an agreement is the natural moment for it. Raised as #6012 with both options and the numbers written up, recorded as an open choice rather than assuming (a). Nothing gets built until you are happy with the approach.

5. Should there be an “End an Exclusive Agreement” button?

For Patrick, Marc and Tommy. There is no such button and no such capability today.

The only ways contract pricing stops are: let it lapse and do not tick extend; edit the end date by hand; or archive the whole customer, which ends all of their agreements at once. So there is a real asymmetry — you can start contract pricing with a button, but you can only stop it by editing a date or by killing the customer record.

The encouraging part: the logic already exists and is already in use. Archiving ends agreements through a routine that handles all three cases properly — a live agreement (end date pulled back to today, auto-extend off, review date pulled back), one that has not started yet (auto-extend off, dates untouched), and one already expired but still auto-extending (auto-extend off). A button would be a thin wrapper around code already running in production, not new logic. That makes this cheap.

OptionWhat it doesCost
A. Do nothingKeep editing end dates by hand.Zero — but the asymmetry and the drift both stay
B. End this agreementButton on a single agreement, reusing the archive routine.Small
C. End all contract pricingCustomer-level — what archive does, without archiving.Small, same code
D. B plus clear the tickAlso unticks Exclusive Contract and pushes it to NetSuite.Small–medium
Recommendation: D, built as B. Ending an agreement is the only moment when the system knows for certain that pricing stopped on purpose — so it is the right place to clear the tick. Doing B without the untick just relocates the drift rather than fixing it.

Two questions for Marc and Tommy

  1. When we end a customer’s exclusive agreement early, should Cloud also clear their Exclusive Contract tick automatically and push that to NetSuite immediately? And do we want a reason or note recorded against the ending, for audit or for finance?
  2. Should ending an agreement warn us if the customer currently has kegs on hire under that pricing — the way archiving already warns — or just end it?

A view on the first: yes to clearing the tick and syncing it, and yes to a note. The note costs almost nothing to add now and is very hard to retrofit once there is a year of endings with no explanation.

7. The engineering record

One discovery issue, opened 17 August, answered with a 50,000-character written investigation (PR #5934) on 25 August. It surfaced nine code discrepancies, filed as seven issues.

#5927 — Archived and on-hold customers could still be given orders Fixed

The only guard applied to rental orders only, and sat on three deprecated endpoints. The ordering screen the application actually uses had no check at all — an operator could place an order for an on-hold customer, or clone an order onto an archived one, through the live interface.

Closed 26 August · PR #5940 (+489/−23, 7 files) · Deliberately partial: rental, lease and refill blocked; collections and transfers still allowed so the fleet can be recovered.

#5928 — One customer could read another customer’s contract Security

The endpoint returning an exclusive agreement checked permissions against the organisation named in the request, never the one that owns the agreement. Any ordinary user at any customer could supply their own organisation plus an arbitrary agreement reference and receive someone else’s contract: term dates, minimum keg rental, maximum monthly supply percentage, annual review date, casual status and the full SKU pricing list.

Closed 27 August · PR #5939 · Returns “not found” rather than “forbidden”, so the endpoint does not confirm another tenant’s agreement exists. The list endpoint was never affected.

#5929 — Two pricing agreements could cover the same day Fixed

The overlap check tested whether either end of a new date range fell inside an existing agreement, but not whether the new range fully enclosed an existing one. A year-long agreement would be accepted over an existing three-month one sitting inside it, and which priced an order was decided by a tiebreak rather than by intent. The same flawed check was duplicated in the front-end form.

Closed 26 August · PR #5938 (+387/−5, 5 files) · No database constraint backs this, so the fix had to land on both sides.

#5930 — Archive silently set the hold flag Fixed

The source of the 220-customer drift in Part 6. Archiving flipped the account-hold flag through a plain setter that raised no event, and un-archiving never cleared it. The two disagreed permanently, and nobody could tell a real credit hold from an archiving side effect.

Closed 27 August · PR #5942 (+218/−12, 8 files) · Deliberately merged after #5927 — removing the implicit hold before the real order guard existed would have removed the incidental protection it was accidentally providing.

#5931 — None of this was documented for users Fixed

The organisations user guide was effectively silent: Account Status appeared only in a screenshot’s alt text; Archive, Hold Account and Agreements were named once as button labels in a single sentence; and the word “Exclusive” did not appear anywhere in the user guide at all. Because the guides had just been surfaced inside the application itself, that silence had become customer-facing.

Closed 25 August · PR #5937 · Satisfies the “raise the gap rather than archive and hope” acceptance criterion on #5846.

#5932 — There is no single “customer stopped trading” action Open — needs a decision

The root finding, and the origin of everything in Parts 1 and 2. Archive was never designed as offboarding; it was simply the closest thing available, which is why it kept being asked to do work it was not built for. Completing the action (#5991) addressed the mechanics. What remains open is the product question: should archived customers be hidden from the organisations list by default, behind a “show archived” toggle?

Open · A decision, not a task — it needs Patrick and Marc, not a developer.

#5933 — One bad row could take the customer list down Fixed

The exclusivity flag was declared in code as always having a value, while the database allowed it to be empty. Because it is read while building the whole customer list, a single producer with an empty flag would have failed the entire list for every user. Confirmed latent — no production row was in that state — but reachable through the customer-update endpoint with a well-formed request.

Closed 29 August · PRs #5941 then #5972 · Split across two releases on purpose: reject bad values first, tighten the column second, so code and schema were never in disagreement. This is the change that normalised the 878 blank Exclusive ticks.

#5991 — Complete the Archive action Shipped in v1.90.0

Scoped from the discovery in #5932 and delivered inside a single sprint. Extends the existing Archive button so it ends agreements, clears the Exclusive tick and pushes it to NetSuite, warns on kegs, refuses on open orders, adds the Account Status filter, and revokes archived-customer access. No new button, no new domain term — everything in Part 1.

Closed 31 August · PR #5992 · Location restore was explicitly held out of scope and is now #6003.

Why this one is worth reading twice

A guess became a receiptThe answer given in the meeting was openly a guess — archive was assumed to pull customers out of estimations and costing. Writing it down properly, with line references, turned an assumption into seven tickets, one security fix, and a completed feature inside two weeks.
The dialog lied, and nobody had checkedThree of the four promises in the archive confirmation box were false. That text had been in front of operators for years. User-facing copy is not covered by any test we run.
The right answer was to slow down firstThe issue asked for a bulk archive. Running it in August would have attached live contract pricing to dead customers at scale. Fixing the action first means the sweep is now safe to run.
