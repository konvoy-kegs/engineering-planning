Milestone 24 Review & Milestone 25 Plan

Sprint ran 3 – 14 August 2026 · Milestone 24 closes 17 August · Team of 3 · Prepared for the CEO & COO

This was the catch-up sprint we planned, and it did its job. Milestone 24 was set up as “catchup and cleanup” — work through the backlog of old issues, clear out the feature-flag estate, and scope the next PHP upgrade. All three landed. 29 issues and 34 pull requests closed across the fortnight, shipped in four releases — plus a further 37 infrastructure items that ran in parallel and are counted separately, because they sit in different repositories.

The biggest single win was billing correctness. Five separate defects in the fuel levy were found and fixed — including one where the platform would silently bill zero if no rate was in effect, with no alert and no trace. That one had no visible symptom until an invoice came out wrong.

The honest caveat: the headline completion figure looks weaker than last sprint (69% against Milestone 23’s 100%), but the two are not measuring the same thing. Milestone 24’s remaining 28 items include four long-running epics and several tracking issues that were never fortnight-sized work. That bookkeeping problem is being fixed this week — see Cleaning up the issue list below.

Shipped

Four releases went out during the sprint — v1.89.28 (3 Aug), v1.89.29 (6 Aug), v1.89.30 (10 Aug) and v1.89.31 (16 Aug). The release cadence that was flagged as a risk in the last two reports is now steady: four releases in a fortnight, against a single release across the whole of the preceding month.

The application side of the release debt is cleared. The infrastructure side is not — kubernetes #475 (develop → main reconcile) is still open and still overdue.

What we shipped

Six themes, each with what it means in practice.

1. The fuel levy now bills correctly, and finance can see it Done

Five defects, found together and fixed together. The platform could silently bill zero when no rate was in effect, with no alert and no trace (#5795). Rate uniqueness for national rates could not be enforced, so duplicates were possible (#5794). The admin page hid future-dated and superseded rates, making scheduled changes invisible to the people managing them (#5793). NetSuite invoice lines carried no description, so finance could not tell what the levy line was (#5798). And the AU/NZ rate history in production was restored after in-place edits had overwritten it (#5786).

Why it matters: this is money going out on customer invoices. A silent zero has no symptom — nothing breaks, nothing alerts, the invoice is simply wrong. It is now alerted and traceable.

2. A security hole in account deletion, closed Done

Soft-deleted users kept working API access when their token carried a UUID subject (#5714) — meaning a deactivated account could still reach the API. Alongside it: signing into a second account no longer leaves the previous user’s identity in place, the app stops telling people “something went wrong” when their session has simply ended (#5712), and a 401 now records what the API actually said, so the next one is diagnosable (#5723).

Why it matters: when we deactivate a user — a departing staff member, a closed customer account — access needs to actually stop. It now does.

3. The feature-flag estate cleaned out Done

A stated goal of the sprint. Seven LaunchDarkly flags with zero code references were archived (#5624), the 2023-vintage KEG-2601 flag pair on the cycles page was removed outright (#5625), and the documentation that described the old behaviour was corrected in two places (#5629, #5731). The remaining flag work is now grouped under its own milestone, LaunchDarkly Feature Flags, with six open items.

Why it matters: every stale flag is a branch of code that might run and nobody remembers why. Fewer flags means faster, safer releases — which is the point of the exercise.

4. Orders and refills correctness Done

Two API endpoints disagreed about whether an empty completion date was valid (#5694); a required field on retrospective orders was being accepted and then ignored (#5693); the Edit Order screen offered a refill pricing change the API then refused with a 403 (#5711). Bulk refills upload gained multi-SKU External ID grouping and an optional Venue column (#5717), and the assets map stopped running a full-fleet count on every single visit to populate one tooltip (#5735).

Why it matters: these are the small contradictions that generate support tickets — a button that shouldn’t be there, a field that quietly does nothing.

5. Test and developer infrastructure Done

The automated end-to-end suite now runs nightly, producing a soak signal rather than only firing on demand (#5611). The Rainforest test inventory was captured before that account was cancelled (#5617) — a small piece of work that prevents permanently losing what those tests covered. Repository hygiene cleared two committed build artefacts (#5728, #5737) and a standard editor setup was added for developers (#5763).

Why it matters: the migration off paid manual testing completes only when the replacement runs unattended and we can prove it caught something.

6. The next platform upgrade is fully scoped Planned

The third stated goal. The PHP 8.5 upgrade (#5665) has been broken down from an idea into 10 sized tickets — version pins and CI, trait binding order, float rounding, deprecation sweep, test-double replacement, container tooling, and the NetSuite URL handling change. Nothing has been built yet; the point of the exercise was to make the work knowable before committing to it.

Why it matters: last year’s framework upgrade ran long because it was scoped as it went. This one is costed up front, so it can be scheduled against real capacity.

Infrastructure: exiting two AWS surcharges

This ran in parallel with everything above and does not appear in any of the numbers on this page — it lives in the terraform and kubernetes repositories, while the Milestone 24 counts cover the konvoy application repository only.

A further 37 items closed during the same fortnight, across two programmes with money attached. AWS applies an Extended Support surcharge once a database engine or Kubernetes version falls out of standard support. It is automatic, open-ended, and buys nothing. No action is required to be charged; action is required to avoid it — which is what made both jobs urgent rather than merely worthwhile.

EKS. All four clusters moved Kubernetes 1.33 → 1.34 on 8–9 August, dev first behind a soak gate, then the three production regions. On 10 August the billing line was confirmed at $0.00 by measurement rather than projection (terraform #594), and the epic closed the same day.

RDS. The production database upgrade itself ran on 2 August and was covered in the last report. What landed this sprint was the close-out and four fixes to the database refresh pipeline, which had been quietly producing MySQL 8.0-shaped clones — a back door that would have reintroduced the old engine into non-production environments.

And a guardrail. A monthly support-lifecycle watch now tracks every cluster and database engine against its support end date (terraform #575). It has already fired once, flagging the next deadline 112 days ahead.

The December deadline. Kubernetes 1.34 leaves standard support on 2 December 2026, and the $2,147/month surcharge returns in full unless all four clusters are on 1.35 by then. The saving is banked but fixed-term. The 1.35 upgrade is currently blocked with a target of 18 November — two weeks of margin against a hard billing date. Full detail, including everything still open, is on the AWS Extended Support exit page.

Cleaning up the issue list

A bookkeeping change with a real effect on how these reports read.

Milestone 24 shows 28 items still open. That number is misleading, and it is worth explaining once because it will keep distorting the completion figure otherwise.

Four of those open items are epics — containers holding a dozen or more child tickets each. An epic sitting in a fortnight’s milestone can never close within that fortnight, so it counts against us every sprint it survives. Several more are long-lived tracking and question items in the same position.

The fix, started this week, is to give each large epic its own milestone so its children group together and stop competing with fortnight-sized work in the sprint list. Two are done — Konvoy End-User Documentation (12 issues) and the Vercel setup audit — alongside the LaunchDarkly Feature Flags milestone already in place. The PHP 8.5 epic is the obvious next candidate: all 10 of its tickets currently sit in no milestone at all.

Note for comparison: because of this, Milestone 24’s 69% and Milestone 23’s 100% are not directly comparable. From Milestone 25 onward the sprint milestones should contain only fortnight-sized work, and the percentage becomes meaningful again.

Six things to watch

Each of these needs a decision rather than an update.

1. The EKS saving expires on 2 December

Kubernetes 1.34 leaves standard support that day and the $2,147 AUD/month surcharge returns in full unless all four clusters are on 1.35. The upgrade (terraform #596) is currently blocked, targeted at 18 November — two weeks of margin against a hard billing date, over a period that includes end-of-year change freezes.

Decision: confirm the November target is real and protect the capacity for it now, not in October.

2. Milestone 25 does not exist yet

The sprint closes today and there is no milestone open for the next one. The three milestones currently open are all thematic — LaunchDarkly, End-User Documentation, Vercel — with no fortnightly container for general work.

Decision: open Milestone 25 with dates before the next sprint starts, and agree its three themes.

3. The infrastructure release debt is still open

The application side cleared with v1.89.28 and has stayed clear across four releases. The infrastructure reconcile (kubernetes #475) has been open across three sprints now. It was flagged in the last report and has not moved.

Decision: schedule it into Milestone 25 explicitly, or accept it as ongoing and stop reporting it as a risk.

4. Carry-over is the highest it has been recently

24 issues carry forward. Four are epics and several are tracking items, so the true figure is nearer 17 — but that is still high against a team of three, and some items have now survived several sprints.

Decision: review the carry-over list and close or backlog anything that has been carried three times.

5. Two production faults are open and unscheduled

Product image responses are corrupted by a PHP deprecation notice written into the response body (#5747), and the metrics endpoint returns a 500 when the Prometheus Redis store is unavailable (#5758). Both are live; neither is assigned to a milestone.

Decision: these should go into Milestone 25 at the top, not carry again.

6. Booths is still waiting on Booths

Unchanged from the last two reports. Phase 0 is coordination on the Booths side (#5051) and the dependent decisions (#5688, #5707) cannot be made without it.

Decision: set a date by which we escalate commercially, or park the epic and stop carrying it.
