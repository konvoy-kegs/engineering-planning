Databricks Decommission — Completion Report

Cost programme board 17 · 25 August – 15 October 2026 · Prepared for the CEO & COO · 16 October 2026 · All figures AUD
Complete · 34 of 34 items done · closed 15 October

Databricks is gone. Konvoy ran three Databricks data-analytics workspaces, plus the private networks, storage and data feed that supported them. Between 25 August and 15 October all of it was removed. Nothing in production depended on it: the only thing that read from it was a fleet-analytics dashboard in Konvoy Cloud, switched off on 2 October, and there has not been a single customer or support signal since.

The saving is about $1,884 a month, roughly $22,600 a year. Databricks cost $1,583 in August. It has cost $0 a day every day since 13 October. Add the AWS networking and storage that sat underneath it and the total comes to about $1,884 a month. This is on top of the June cost plan’s target — that plan had set Databricks aside as “strategic, not in scope” — and it needed no new contract, no exit fee and no receiver sign-off: the subscription was pay-as-you-go, so the charges stopped when the usage did. The only new cost is about $3 a month to keep archive copies of the data.

The number is the measured one, not the pitch. The August briefing headlined “$1,951 a month” with no currency on it; read as US dollars that is $2,815 and about half too high. Once measured properly in August the projection became $1,883–1,946 a month, and the delivered figure sits at the floor of that range because one component that cannot be separated out on the AWS bill (up to $150 a month of compute) has deliberately not been claimed. November is the first month the bill will show the full effect; a line-by-line reconciliation against the settled October bill is booked for 3 November.

Not claimed, on purpose. Three things also stopped but are left out of the number. First, up to about $150 a month of short-lived compute that Databricks spun up for its jobs: it stopped on 3 October, but AWS has no way to separate it from the rest of our server line, and part of it turned out never to have been Databricks in the first place. Leaving it out is why the result sits at the floor of the projected range rather than the middle. Second, removing the Airbyte data feed freed about 44% of the processor and two-thirds of the memory that had been reserved on the US production cluster. The clusters do not shrink by themselves, so this is not a bill reduction yet — it is headroom that the server right-sizing work can now bank. Third, a handful of small disks and some database log space: real, but too small to matter.

Deliberately kept

A short “retain list” was written before the work started and re-checked before every destructive step.

The shared data-lake storage that other systems use; two access roles belonging to other integrations; the table that holds the NetSuite connection token; and three new deep-archive buckets holding a full copy of every Databricks dataset. The copies were proven by restoring a sample and checking its fingerprint on 8 October, then by a file-by-file coverage check on 12 October. Deep archive has a 180-day minimum and takes 12–48 hours to retrieve from, which is acceptable because no system reads this data; it costs about $3 a month.

Why it matters: if anyone ever needs the historical analytics data, it is all there and recoverable in two days; nothing was lost to get the saving.

How it was done

The rule throughout was reversible first, irreversible last. Nothing was deleted without an archive copy already verified, and 90% of the saving had already stopped on 3 October — twelve days before anything was destroyed.
Preparation25 August

Three deep-archive buckets created so that every later deletion had somewhere to copy to first.

Answer the questions25 Aug – 24 Sep

The oldest, hand-built workspace was archived and deleted on 27 August. Then the three business questions that gated everything else were answered: the one customer organisation still tied to the dashboard turned out to be Konvoy’s own internal account; there is no live Snowflake contract reading the data lake; and the Databricks subscription is pay-as-you-go with no end date, so no receiver sign-off was needed. A cost baseline was snapshotted before anything changed.

A safety defect found on the way — manually-triggered deployment runs could apply changes without a confirmation step across 26 workflows — was fixed at the shared component so it is closed everywhere.

Untie the app24 Sep – 6 Oct

Konvoy Cloud was changed to run without its Databricks settings and released to all three production regions on 25 September. The fleet-analytics dashboard was switched off on 2 October — day zero — and the five Databricks secrets were removed from the app on 6 October.

Pause everything23 Sep – 12 Oct

All jobs, pipelines and query engines were stopped on 3 October and the four Airbyte connections paused — nothing deleted, everything restartable. Then a seven-day observation window: no activity of any kind from 4 to 10 October, the production app re-tested, zero Databricks errors in production monitoring, no support contact. A written GO was recorded on 12 October. In the same window the remaining storage was copied to deep archive and the copies tested.

Destroy12 – 15 Oct

Data tables deleted and storage emptied on 12 October. On 15 October the two remaining workspaces were destroyed, then the shared Databricks infrastructure — 69 of 69 components — from a freshly reviewed plan checked against the retain list, followed by the stored secrets, the workflows and the Airbyte deployment on both clusters. Each step was read back from AWS afterwards to confirm it was gone.

Close out15 Oct → 3 Nov

Networks confirmed gone. The epic was closed on 15 October and the reconciliation of the settled October bill against this report is booked for 3 November.

Recorded for the audit trail

Five things were written down on the closing record rather than resolved. None affects the saving.
  1. The archive of the oldest workspace holds 167,484 files against an August estimate of 176,842, although the total size agrees. No file list was taken before that storage was deleted, so the gap can be explained (normal churn between the estimate and the copy) but not proven.
  2. A small folder of data with no registered owner (7,648 files, 79 MB) was found in the production catalogue. It was archived and the original left in place in the shared data lake.
  3. The production catalogue was force-deleted with a few leftover registrations still attached; the development one had a stale link to a workspace that no longer existed.
  4. One legacy network had already disappeared by 3 October. This programme did not remove it and the cause is not known.
  5. Two process notes. The Airbyte removal was run on the general instruction to complete the board rather than a separate per-step go; and two small clean-up pull requests were raised by the AI agent and merged while still marked as awaiting review.
Why it matters: these are the honest edges of the work. They are recorded so that nobody discovers them later and wonders why they were not mentioned.
