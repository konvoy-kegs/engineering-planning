Engineering Output 2024 → 2026

The same six months — April to September — compared across three years · All 36 active konvoy-kegs repositories · Claude Team plan in use since 1 May 2026 · Prepared for the CEO & COO · 12 October 2026

The team shipped more in April–September 2026 than in the same six months of either previous year, with fewer people. 852 pull requests were merged, against 587 in 2024 and 451 in 2025 — up 45% and 89% respectively. The code changed grew with it (721k lines versus 535k and 405k), so this is not the same work cut into smaller pieces: the typical pull request in 2026 is actually larger than before.

Per person, output is about two and a half times what it was. Four engineers did almost all of the 2026 work (a fifth left in May); seven and five were active in 2024 and 2025. Measured per contributor-month, the team merged 37 pull requests for every month a person was active in 2026, against 15 in 2024 and 16 in 2025. Inside 2026 the step is sharp: 12 per contributor-month in January–April, 41 from May, the month the Claude Team plan started. Millicent, the one engineer present in all three years, went from about 16 pull requests a month in 2024 and 2025 to 28 a month since May.

The honest part. Faster output has come with less human review — 56% of 2026 pull requests were looked at by a second person, down from 79% in 2024 — and with 57% of the merged work coming from one person. Bug-fix pull requests are a bigger share of the total, the issue backlog is growing faster than it is being closed, and fewer production releases went out than in 2025. None of these undo the result; they are the costs to manage so it holds. Details and the decisions they point to are in Things to watch.

Things to watch

The costs that came with the speed. Each ends with what would help.

1. Less of the code is being read by a second person

79% of pull requests had a review from someone other than the author in 2024, 67% in 2025, 56% in 2026. GitHub Copilot now comments on 94% of pull requests, which is useful but is not the same as a colleague reading it. With one author producing over half the volume, the review load lands on the other three.

What would help: agree a rule for which changes must have a human approval (anything touching billing, auth, infrastructure, data) and let the rest go on automated review. Nothing from you beyond backing that rule.

2. Over half the output comes from one person

Rob authored 483 of the 852 pull requests in the window (57%). That is the Head of Engineering doing most of the building. It is why the numbers moved, and it is also the single biggest dependency in the team.

What would help: a decision on whether that is the intended shape of the team for 2027, or whether the Claude workflow should be deliberately spread so Bea and Millicent carry more of it. Flagging it so it is a choice, not a surprise.

3. Bug fixes are a bigger slice, and the backlog is growing

Fix-type pull requests were 27% of the total in 2024, 25% in 2025 and 41% in 2026. 1,338 issues were opened in the window and 929 closed, so the open list grew by about 409. The likeliest reading is that far more is being captured now (customer feedback, Sentry errors, the Billing test rounds) rather than that more is breaking — but this data cannot tell the two apart.

What would help: nothing from you yet. Engineering will report production error rates from Sentry alongside this next quarter so the question has an answer.

4. More merged, fewer releases

Konvoy Cloud had 48 tagged production releases in the 2025 window and 27 in 2026. Code is reaching the main branch faster than it is reaching customers — the same pattern the M32 report raised, where the Billing work was merged but not live.

What would help: a standing release cadence (weekly, say) so merged work does not queue. That is an engineering decision; it is here so the release count is read correctly.

5. Pull requests are bigger and wait longer

The median pull request grew from 99 lines (2024) and 46 (2025) to 237 in 2026, and the median wait from opening to merging went from 3.7 h and 1.3 h to 13.7 h. Larger changes that sit in a queue are where review is hardest and mistakes hide.

What would help: nothing from you — it follows from items 1 and 4 and should improve with them.

Method and caveats

What was counted

Every pull request merged and every issue closed in any konvoy-kegs repository, pulled from the GitHub API on 12 October 2026 (search by merge date and close date, one calendar month at a time; 1,951 merged PRs and 933 closed issues in the three windows, plus 994 PRs for the surrounding months). Commits were read from the default branch of all 36 repositories pushed to since April 2024. Reviews were read per pull request to separate human from automated reviewers.

What was excluded

Pull requests and commits by bots: Dependabot, Copilot's coding agent, GitHub Actions (1,402 / 908 / 568 image-bump commits in the Kubernetes repository), the Cowork daily digest, and Sentry. Merge commits. Reviews by copilot-pull-request-reviewer are reported separately rather than counted as human review.

What the numbers cannot say

Pull requests and lines of code measure activity, not value or quality; they are presented together with PR size, review coverage and fix share precisely so that one cannot be read without the others. Direct attribution of work to Claude is not possible from GitHub: only 173 commits in the 2026 window carry an explicit Claude co-author line, most of them automated. The before/after comparison rests on timing and on the per-person control, not on tagging. Team composition changed every year; the contributor-month normalisation and the single-engineer control are the two corrections applied for that.

Source: GitHub GraphQL and REST APIs for the konvoy-kegs organisation, retrieved 12 October 2026. Windows are 1 April–30 September inclusive, by merge date (PRs) and close date (issues), UTC. Lead times are opened→merged for PRs and opened→closed-as-completed for issues. Contributor-month = one person merging at least one PR in a calendar month.
