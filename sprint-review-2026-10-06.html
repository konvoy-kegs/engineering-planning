Milestone 32 Review & M33 Plan

Sprint ran 15 September – 6 October 2026 (planned to 28 September, extended one week) · Next sprint M33, 7 – 19 October · Team of 3 · Prepared for the CEO & COO · 6 October 2026

The sprint set three goals and finished all three. Milestone 32 was written up as “Billing in place ready for production, Vercel all cleaned up, LaunchDarkly all cleaned up.” The LaunchDarkly clean-up epic closed on 25 September (18 of 18 items), the Vercel audit epic closed on 2 October (10 of 10), and the Billing epic merged to the main branch at 11:19 this morning — 55 commits, 267 files, 40 of its 42 items done. Two further epics closed alongside — credit notes, with a deliberate decision to view them rather than create them, and the test-quality audit — and the API-documentation migration is ten of fourteen done. The extra week bought the Billing merge; without it the sprint would have closed at roughly the same percentage with Billing still on a branch.

By the numbers it is the strongest close since July. Milestone 32 finished at 90% (106 of 118 items) against 56% last time. Across all milestones the team closed 107 issues and merged 100 pull requests in the three weeks, split almost evenly between the three engineers. The one caveat on the figure: this was a 22-day sprint, not a 12-day one, so the throughput per week is in line with the last two sprints rather than above them.

The honest part is what is not in production. Only one release went out in the window (v1.90.2 on 25 September). Everything merged since — 62 pull requests including Billing and the fix for the LaunchDarkly add-on that was sending login passwords to a third party in plain text — is on the main branch and nowhere else. Worse, the automatic deploys to the dev-oc test environment had been failing since 6 September and nobody noticed until Billing needed to land there this morning; it was fixed at 13:15 today. Two decisions for this session: cut a release this week so the security fixes go live, and name who books the Billing walkthrough with Finance — the page cannot reach a customer until that sign-off exists.

Not yet released

v1.90.2 (25 September) is the only release in the window and nothing has shipped since. It carried the staff-instructions API, the Locations search box, the automatic reload of stale browser bundles, the fuel-levy removal from the order-detail page, the shell-script lint gate, the LaunchDarkly SDK bump and the new production Content-Security-Policy — fixes and groundwork, no database migrations, no feature turned on.

Waiting on the main branch, unreleased: the whole Billing epic (#6361, merged today), the LaunchDarkly recording fix and add-on removal (#6302, #6331), the backend Sentry redaction (#6323), the staff pre-fill link fix (#6246), the Brick Lane term-rental count (#6320), the search-stopword fix (#6232) and the rental notification crash (#6341). Release dates this quarter so far: 31 August, 14 September, 25 September.

The dev-oc deploy pipeline was red from 6 September to this morning (#6384): new upstream dependency advisories failed the audit gate on main while every pull request stayed green. It is the same failure shape as the four-day block reported last time, except it lasted a month. Fixed by #6390; see watch item 2.

What we finished

Six themes, in the order a manager should care about them. “In v1.90.2” means live; “On main” means merged and waiting for the next release.

1. Billing in Konvoy Cloud is built and merged — UAT and release are what remain On main

Patrick’s priority #1 from the August planning session. A customer can now see every invoice and credit note NetSuite holds for them inside Konvoy Cloud — number, date, amount, balance due, status — filter by date, status and trading entity, and download the PDF. A Konvoy staff member can open the same page for any customer they are authorised for, and email a document to the customer’s billing address with one click (#5835, #5842, #6337, #6346). The epic branch merged to main this morning as one pull request (#6361) with no database changes.

Four decisions were taken that shape what customers get. The PDF is the exact document NetSuite prints, fetched through a small script deployed inside NetSuite, rather than a Konvoy-drawn copy that could differ from the one Finance sent (#5837, #6047) — all six invoice templates in use, across five legal entities, were proven to print on 4 October. Statuses use NetSuite’s own words so the page never disagrees with a statement (#6304). Customers who trade with both Konvoy Australia and Konvoy Services see both entities’ documents in one list with an entity filter — 305 customers are in that position (#5838). And the page reads NetSuite through a dedicated read-only credential, so a customer-facing screen can never write to the ledger (#5853).

Credit notes: view, don’t create. Two weeks of work to let staff raise and approve credit memos from Konvoy Cloud was built, then deliberately removed before it reached any shared environment (#4315, #6293, 9,312 lines). The decision on 27 September: Finance raises credit notes in NetSuite; Konvoy Cloud shows them. The code is recoverable if that changes. The scare from last report — Konvoy Services invoices printing from the wrong template, “57 of 2,370” — turned out to be a false alarm once the templates were actually rendered (#6119). A user guide for the section is written (#6368).

What is not done, in order: deploy to dev-oc and connect it to production NetSuite for one or two named customers (#6115 — Finance names the customers); the walkthrough and written sign-off from Patrick, Mariana, Rose, Marie Anne and Alice (#5844 — nobody has booked it); a production release; then the customer-facing switch, which is off in production and stays off until someone decides which customers see it first (#5843). The staff view goes live for operator admins with the release, no switch needed. One small infrastructure change must go first or the email button silently drops mail (terraform #752).

Why it matters: this removes the “can you send me a copy of invoice X” email from Finance’s inbox and the NetSuite login from Sales’ day. It is also the first time customers see financial data in Konvoy Cloud, which is why the read-only credential and the UAT gate are not optional. Entity names on the page are the legal names, which for Konvoy Services include “(Receivers and Managers Appointed)” — Patrick needs to confirm the wording before customers see it (#6040).

2. LaunchDarkly cleaned up: 20 flags became 9, six orphaned servers went, one serious leak found Epic closed 25 Sept

The flag-lifecycle epic (#5630) started in July with 20 live feature flags, nine of which nothing in the code read any more. It closed with 9 flags, every one read by code and tagged for monitoring, and a written rule that a gated feature stays behind its switch rather than being deleted — learned the hard way when one deletion had to be reverted (#6086). The fuel-levy line on the order-detail page was the one deliberate removal: no customer had ever been shown it (#5692).

Six LaunchDarkly relay servers were found running in our clusters serving no one — one of them for two and a half years, two in environments decommissioned in May. Each held permanent connections to LaunchDarkly and its own Redis. All six were destroyed, verified across all four clusters (#5732, terraform #723, terraform #728). The LaunchDarkly browser SDK was moved off a version that reached end-of-life on 30 September, five days before the deadline (#6236). And the asset-cycle dashboard flag was switched off in production on 23 September with Patrick’s and Mark’s approval, after seven days of logs showed zero customer traffic — that is what started the Databricks decommission clock (#5915).

The leak. Auditing what the LaunchDarkly add-ons actually recorded showed the “observability” package was sending every browser request body to LaunchDarkly verbatim — login passwords, refresh tokens and password-reset tokens, in plain text (#6090, #6107). Nobody used the feature and nobody knew it was on. Recording was switched off (#6302, 30 Sept) and both add-ons were removed outright (#6271, 2 Oct), proven in a browser by capturing the traffic before and after. Both fixes are on main, not in production — see watch item 1. How long it had been on is not established; it was already running when first checked on 10 August.

Why it matters: fewer switches that can be set wrong in production; six servers’ worth of compute and Redis that cost money for nothing; a supported SDK. The leak is the one that matters: passwords do not expire the way tokens do. Nothing is known to be compromised, and the decision taken was not to ask LaunchDarkly to delete what it already holds. Two loose ends: the two server-side LaunchDarkly keys sitting in plain text in the terraform repository still need rotating (terraform #730), and every logged-in user’s name and email still go to LaunchDarkly on each flag check (#6270, in progress).

3. Vercel audit closed: analytics that work, headers that protect, tokens that stay home Epic closed 2 Oct

Rob’s August audit of the Vercel hosting setup (#5754) found that Web Analytics and Speed Insights had been switched on for fifteen months and had collected nothing — a missing environment variable and an uninstalled package. Both now collect (#5657; 216 visitors and 1,632 page views in the first week measured). Browser security headers — HSTS, Referrer-Policy, Permissions-Policy — have been live since 16 August (#5658). Google Tag Manager, a third party able to inject any script into the app, was removed outright. A Content-Security-Policy is deployed in report-only mode; making it enforcing is the one item split out and still open (#6043).

The audit then became a leak hunt. Single-use sign-up and password-reset links carried their token in the page address, so the token reached whoever saw the address: Vercel Analytics first (#5726 — counted four times, five tokens, all expired by 20 August, none known used), then Sentry’s error reports and session replays (#6092, #6091), then the staff “pre-fill customer form” link (#6221), and finally LaunchDarkly (theme 2). Each path is now closed: tokens travel in headers, the address bar is scrubbed before any monitoring starts, and both Sentry ends redact the token routes. Three smaller wins: browsers no longer run bundles months old (one had been seven months stale), the public sign-up form recovers from a stale deploy instead of crashing, and a force-push can no longer leave a stale preview that passes review (#6159, #6160, #6206).

Why it matters: three fewer third parties hold customer data (GTM, Google Analytics, two LaunchDarkly add-ons), the analytics the business pays for finally produce numbers, and the token exposure was bounded and is structurally closed rather than merely quiet. Two accepted leftovers, so they are not a surprise later: the five old token rows stay in Vercel’s analytics for a year, and the page addresses still carry the token — the durable redesign was sized too large and shelved.

4. The API documentation now comes from the code, not from hand-written notes On a branch, 10 of 14

Konvoy’s public API reference (the one on konvoykegs.com) was generated from 267 files of hand-written annotations, with the same twenty-odd parameters copied into every endpoint and six endpoints documented at the wrong address. Millicent’s epic (#6255) replaces that with a generator that reads the real routes and request objects, so a change to an endpoint changes its documentation automatically, and every backend pull request now shows reviewers exactly what it changes in the published contract (#6256). The cut-over produced a byte-for-byte identical document (#6261), and the Device, Asset, Order, Collection and User endpoints are migrated.

Why it matters: API consumers get documentation that cannot drift from reality, and a pull request that quietly removes a documented field will fail its checks once the last four items land. The work sits on an epic branch with a draft merge open (#6385); it reaches main next sprint.

5. Fixes customers and support will notice Mostly on main

The Pricing page’s “actual term rentals” for an exclusive agreement whose extension had ended showed roughly zero kegs for Brick Lane; it now counts from agreement start to extension end and shows the correct 26,543 kegs, 102% of their 26,000 minimum — Tommy’s decision (#6320). Global search returned nothing for any venue whose name contained “The” or “and”, raised by Alice (#6232) — fixed. The Locations page gained a search box, the only way to find one venue in a 9,000-row list without paging (#6241, live in v1.90.2). The Rentals download was trimmed from 78 columns to the 14 Alice asked for, one row per SKU (#6186). And a production bug found on 2 October: a rental placed from a location with no organisation crashed the order-placed event, so no Slack message, no notification and no customer email went out — a latent defect since October 2025, now a one-line fix (#6341).

Why it matters: two of these are revenue-reporting corrections (Brick Lane’s contract compliance, the silent notification drop), and all but the Locations search are waiting on a release. One known regression carries: an operator Manager gets a 403 on Notifications (#6231); a fix is open but its cause is a code reading, not yet reproduced.

6. The plumbing: a broken deploy pipeline, an honest test audit, and the tools the team codes with Mixed

Deploys. The dev-oc backend had not deployed from main since 6 September (#6384). The shell scripts the pipeline runs had no lint gate at all — 18 of 19 were never checked (#6190); now they are. Merging an epic branch now closes its issues and marks the board automatically (#6113), after three rounds of fixing the fixer. The NetSuite contract tests now run against a mock NetSuite in CI (#6179), and the frontend test runner moved to Vitest 4 (#6205).

Test quality. A full audit of the ~7,700 automated tests was published (#6275). The suite is mostly sincere, but on the paths where a silent bug costs most it does not check behaviour: 34 assertions that pass on an empty result, six NetSuite invoice tests that test a copy of the code, and 33 frontend tests that only pass when run in a particular order. The fix is twelve small, scheduled slices (#6276–#6287) — none started, and the epic closed itself by accident when the report merged (watch item 4).

Agent tooling. Eight issues went to the Claude Code skills the team writes most code through — the pull-request reviewer, the atomic-commits skill, the read-only NetSuite access skill, the shared engineering log — plus the staff-instructions epic that will host the NetSuite runbooks behind a login (#6161, 1 of 8 done, carries).

Why it matters: at three people, a day lost to a red pipeline or a flaky test is a meaningful share of the sprint. The deploy failure went unnoticed for a month because nothing pages anyone when the test environment stops updating — a cheap fix and the real lesson of this sprint.

Six things to watch

Ordered by how quickly they need a decision.

1. A plaintext-password leak is fixed in code and still live in production

The LaunchDarkly add-on that sent login passwords and reset tokens to a third party (#6090) was switched off on 30 September and removed on 2 October. The last release was 25 September. Until the next one is published, the production app is still running the old bundle and still sending them. The same release carries the backend Sentry redaction and the staff-link token fix.

Decision: cut the release this week and treat it as the first item of M33. The rule proposed in the last two reports — a cut release publishes within two working days or someone says why not — would have had this live a week ago.

2. The test environment silently stopped updating for a month

Every merge to main deploys automatically to dev-oc, where Finance will do the Billing walkthrough. The backend half of that deploy failed on every run from 6 September to this morning (#6384) because new dependency advisories tripped the audit gate on main while pull requests stayed green. Nothing alerts anyone when a deploy fails, so the first person to notice was the one trying to land Billing there today. Last report flagged the same mechanism as a four-day block; this time it was 30 days.

Decision: a failed deploy to any environment posts to Slack and is cleared the same day. And separate the audit gate’s “new upstream advisory” failures from the deploy so a vendor CVE cannot stop the test environment.

3. Billing cannot reach a customer until Finance is in the room

Three of the four remaining steps belong outside Engineering: Finance names the one or two customers whose real NetSuite data the test environment may read; Patrick confirms the legal-entity wording customers will see (#6040); and the walkthrough with Patrick, Mariana, Rose, Marie Anne and Alice (#5844) has no booking and no named owner for making it. The 30 September scope date passed; the work is done, the sign-off is not.

Decision: who books the walkthrough, and for which day in the week of 12 October. Rob can have dev-oc ready within two days of the customer names.

4. The test-quality epic closed itself with none of its work done

The audit report merged on 28 September with a closing keyword in the commit, so GitHub closed the epic (#6275) although its own pull request says “stays open”. Its twelve remediation slices are all open, in no milestone. The findings are not cosmetic: 33 frontend tests pass only in declared order, and the keg-replay, device-message and authorisation paths have the thinnest checks in the suite.

Decision: reopen the epic and schedule the two High frontend slices in M33, or park the whole thing explicitly with a date. It should not sit closed with 0 of 12 done.

5. The EKS 1.35 decision is past its date

Kubernetes 1.34 leaves AWS standard support on 2 December; after that AWS either upgrades the control plane for us, leaving the rest behind, or starts the Extended Support meter again — the one worth $2,147 AUD a month that was removed in August. The programme page said decide by 30 September. The prerequisite chain is four components and three production clusters; it fits in two sprints if it starts now.

Decision: commit to the 1.35 path in M33, starting with the ingress-nginx bumps on prod eu → na → oc. Detail in the cost report.

6. Two accepted exposures, so they are not a surprise later

Rob decided not to ask Vercel to delete the five expired token rows in its analytics, or LaunchDarkly to delete the request bodies it already holds — in both cases the effort was judged larger than the residual risk, and LaunchDarkly offers no self-serve deletion. Nothing is known to be compromised. Both decisions are written on the issues (#5726, #6090).

Nothing from you — unless you would rather the deletion requests were sent anyway, in which case say so and they will be.
