Milestone 32 Review & M33 Plan

Sprint ran 15 September – 6 October 2026 (extended one week) · Next sprint M33, 7 – 19 October · Team of 3 · Prepared for the CEO & COO · 6 October 2026

The sprint set three goals and finished all three. Billing ready for production, Vercel cleaned up, LaunchDarkly cleaned up. The LaunchDarkly clean-up closed on 25 September, the Vercel audit on 2 October, and Billing merged into the main codebase this morning. The extra week bought that merge. Two more epics closed alongside: credit notes (with a decision to show them, not create them) and the test-quality audit.

By the numbers it is the strongest close since July. Milestone 32 finished at 90%, against 56% last time. The team closed 107 issues and merged 100 pull requests in three weeks, split almost evenly between the three engineers. Allow for the sprint being a week longer than usual and the pace is in line with recent sprints rather than above them.

The honest part is what is not in production. Only one release went out (25 September). Everything since — including Billing and the fix for a LaunchDarkly add-on that was sending login passwords to a third party in plain text — is merged but not live. And the automatic deploys to the test environment had been failing since 6 September without anyone noticing, until Billing needed to land there this morning. Two decisions for this session: cut a release this week so the security fixes go live, and name who books the Billing walkthrough with Finance.

Not yet released

v1.90.2 on 25 September is the only release in the window. It carried fixes and groundwork — the Locations search box, automatic reload of stale browser bundles, the shell-script lint gate, the new security policy — with no database changes and nothing switched on.

Merged since and waiting: the whole Billing epic, the LaunchDarkly recording fix and add-on removal, the Sentry token redaction, the staff pre-fill link fix, the Brick Lane term-rental count, the search fix for names containing “The”, and the rental-notification crash. Release dates this quarter: 31 August, 14 September, 25 September.

The test-environment deploy pipeline was red from 6 September to this morning: new dependency advisories failed the audit gate on the main branch while every pull request stayed green. Same failure as the four-day block reported last time, except it lasted a month. Fixed today; see watch item 2.

What we finished

Six themes, in the order a manager should care about them. Underlined names link to the GitHub record. “Live” means in production; “Merged” means waiting for the next release.

1. Billing in Konvoy Cloud is built — sign-off and release are what remain Merged

Patrick’s priority #1 from August. A customer can now see every invoice and credit note NetSuite holds for them inside Konvoy Cloud, filter by date, status and trading entity, and download the PDF. Staff can open the same page for any customer and email a document to them with one click. The PDF is the exact document NetSuite prints, not a Konvoy copy that might differ from what Finance sent. Customers who deal with both Konvoy Australia and Konvoy Services see both in one list. The page reads NetSuite through a read-only login, so a customer-facing screen can never change the ledger (the Billing epic).

One scope decision: the ability for staff to raise credit notes from Konvoy Cloud was built and then deliberately removed before it reached any shared environment. Finance raises them in NetSuite; Konvoy Cloud shows them (credit-notes epic). The “wrong invoice template” scare from last report turned out to be a false alarm once the templates were actually printed.

Still to do, in order: connect the test environment to real NetSuite data for one or two customers Finance names; the walkthrough and sign-off with Patrick, Mariana, Rose, Marie Anne and Alice (not yet booked); a release; then switch it on for a first group of customers. Patrick also needs to confirm the legal entity names customers will see, since Konvoy Services’ includes “(Receivers and Managers Appointed)”.

Why it matters: Finance stops forwarding invoice copies by email, Sales stops logging into NetSuite to look one up, and customers help themselves.

2. LaunchDarkly cleaned up: 20 switches became 9, six idle servers removed, one serious leak found Epic closed 25 Sept

Feature switches let us turn a feature on for some customers and not others. In July there were 20 and nine of them did nothing. There are now 9, each one checked and monitored, with a rule that a parked feature stays behind its switch rather than being deleted (the flag clean-up epic). Six LaunchDarkly relay servers were found running in our clusters serving nobody — one for two and a half years — and were removed. The browser SDK was moved off a version that reached end-of-life on 30 September. And the asset-cycle dashboard was switched off with Patrick’s and Mark’s approval after a week of logs showed no customer used it — which started the Databricks shutdown clock.

The leak. Checking what the LaunchDarkly add-ons actually recorded showed one of them was sending every browser request to LaunchDarkly verbatim — including login passwords, refresh tokens and password-reset tokens, in plain text (the LaunchDarkly leak). Nobody used the feature and nobody knew it was on. Recording was switched off and both add-ons removed, proven by capturing the traffic before and after. The fix is merged but not yet released — watch item 1. How long it had been on is not known.

Why it matters: fewer ways to get a release wrong, less paid for nothing, and a leak of customer passwords closed. Nothing is known to be compromised; the decision was taken not to ask LaunchDarkly to delete what it already holds.

3. Vercel audit closed: analytics that work, headers that protect, tokens that stay home Epic closed 2 Oct

Rob’s August audit of the web hosting setup (the Vercel audit epic) found that the web analytics had been switched on for fifteen months and had collected nothing. They now do. Standard browser security headers have been live since mid-August, Google Tag Manager — a third party able to run any script in the app — was removed, and a content-security policy is in place in monitoring mode, with enforcement the one item still open.

The audit then became a leak hunt. Single-use sign-up and password-reset links carried their token in the page address, so the token reached whoever saw the address: our analytics provider first (five tokens, all expired, none known used), then our error-monitoring tool, then a staff link. Each path is now closed. Three smaller wins: browsers no longer run months-old versions of the app, the public sign-up form recovers from a stale deploy instead of crashing, and a quirk that could show reviewers the wrong preview is fixed.

Why it matters: fewer third parties holding customer data, analytics that finally produce numbers, and a token exposure that was small, bounded and is now structurally closed.

4. The API documentation now comes from the code, not from hand-written notes 10 of 14 done

Konvoy’s public API reference was maintained by hand across 267 files, with the same parameters copied into every endpoint and six endpoints documented at the wrong address. Millicent’s epic (API docs migration) replaces that with a generator that reads the real code, so documentation cannot drift from reality, and every backend change now shows reviewers exactly what it alters in the published contract. Half the endpoints are migrated; the work reaches the main branch next sprint.

Why it matters: customers and partners integrating with the API get documentation that is always right, at no ongoing effort.

5. Fixes customers and support will notice Mostly merged

The Pricing page showed roughly zero kegs against Brick Lane’s exclusive agreement after its extension ended; it now shows the correct 26,543 kegs, 102% of their minimum (term-rental count). Global search returned nothing for any venue with “The” or “and” in its name, raised by Alice — fixed. The Locations page gained a search box (live). The Rentals download was trimmed from 78 columns to the 14 Alice asked for. And a rental placed from a location with no organisation was silently sending no Slack message, no notification and no customer email — a defect since October 2025, found and fixed (rental notifications).

Why it matters: two of these are revenue-reporting corrections; all but the Locations search are waiting on a release. One regression carries: operator Managers get an error on Notifications.

6. The plumbing: deploys, an honest test audit, and the tools the team codes with Mixed

The test environment had not received a backend deploy since 6 September. The scripts the pipeline runs had no quality check at all; now they do. Merging a large piece of work now closes its tickets and updates the board automatically. A full audit of the ~7,700 automated tests was published (test-quality audit): the suite is mostly sincere, but on the paths where a silent bug costs most it does not check behaviour, and 33 frontend tests only pass when run in a particular order. The fix is twelve small, scheduled pieces — none started (watch item 4). Eight further issues went to the AI coding tools the team now writes most code through.

Why it matters: at three people, a day lost to a broken pipeline or a flaky test is a real share of the sprint. The deploy failure went unnoticed for a month because nothing tells anyone when the test environment stops updating.

Six things to watch

Ordered by how quickly they need a decision.

1. A plaintext-password leak is fixed in code and still live in production

The LaunchDarkly add-on that sent login passwords and reset tokens to a third party was switched off on 30 September and removed on 2 October. The last release was 25 September, so production is still running the old version and still sending them.

Decision: cut the release this week, as the first item of M33.

2. The test environment silently stopped updating for a month

Every merge deploys automatically to the test environment where Finance will do the Billing walkthrough. The backend half of that failed on every run from 6 September to this morning, and nothing alerts anyone when a deploy fails. Last report flagged the same mechanism as a four-day block; this time it was 30 days.

Decision: a failed deploy to any environment posts to Slack and is cleared the same day.

3. Billing cannot reach a customer until Finance is in the room

Three of the four remaining steps belong outside Engineering: Finance names the customers whose real data the test environment may read, Patrick confirms the legal-entity wording, and the walkthrough has no booking and no owner. The work is done; the sign-off is not.

Decision: who books the walkthrough, and for which day in the week of 12 October.

4. The test-quality epic closed itself with none of its work done

The audit report merged on 28 September and GitHub closed the epic automatically, although its twelve remediation pieces are all open and unscheduled. The findings are not cosmetic.

Decision: reopen it and schedule the two highest-priority pieces in M33, or park it explicitly with a date.

5. The Kubernetes 1.35 decision is past its date

Our Kubernetes version leaves AWS standard support on 2 December. After that AWS either upgrades part of it for us, leaving the rest behind, or the Extended Support surcharge — the $2,147 AUD a month removed in August — comes back. The work fits in two sprints if it starts now.

Decision: commit to the 1.35 path in M33. Detail in the cost report.

6. Two accepted exposures, so they are not a surprise later

Rob decided not to ask Vercel to delete the five expired token records in its analytics, or LaunchDarkly to delete the request data it already holds — in both cases the effort was judged larger than the residual risk. Nothing is known to be compromised. Both decisions are written on the tickets.
